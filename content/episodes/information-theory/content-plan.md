# Information Theory Content Plan

## Purpose and audience
This episode explains why information can be measured in bits. It is designed for curious viewers who know everyday examples of choices and messages but have not studied information theory. The story should make the logarithm feel inevitable through concrete decisions, then connect the intuition to coding, communication, and uncertainty.

## Learning objective
By the end, viewers can explain that information measures a reduction in uncertainty, calculate the information in an equally likely choice as log2(N), and recognize why doubling the number of possibilities adds one bit. The audience should leave with a reusable mental model rather than a memorized equation.

## Factual claims
1. A question with two equally likely answers carries one bit when its answer resolves the uncertainty.
2. N equally likely alternatives carry log2(N) bits of information.
3. Information depends on the distribution of possibilities, so equally likely examples are a deliberate first approximation.
4. A bit is a unit of information, not a physical object or a synonym for a binary digit in every context.
5. Efficient codes assign shorter descriptions to common outcomes and longer descriptions to rare outcomes.

## Narrative arc
Open with a sealed box and a question: how much did the answer tell us? Move from an unresolved set of possibilities to a fair coin flip. Show that one binary question cuts the possibility space in half. Generalize the repeated halving process to powers of two and then to log2(N). Close by connecting the same idea to messages, compression, and noisy channels, while naming the assumptions that made the simple examples work.

## Narration guidance
Use short spoken sentences, concrete nouns, and deliberate pauses around the words uncertainty, answer, and bit. Introduce notation only after the viewer has seen the repeated choice process. Read “log base two of N” once, then reinforce it visually. Avoid claiming that every real-world message has exactly log2(N) bits; explain that the exact quantity depends on probabilities and context.

## Structure and timing
The first minute establishes uncertainty with a hidden object and a two-choice reveal. The second minute compares one, two, four, and eight equally likely possibilities, making each additional question visible. The final minute generalizes to N, shows the logarithm, and connects the result to coding and compression. Leave a short pause after the final equation so the viewer can restate the idea.

## Sources and verification
Use a standard information theory textbook for definitions of self-information and entropy, and verify notation against a university lecture note. Cite the source in production notes even when the final video uses no on-screen bibliography. Check every numerical example with a calculator and ensure that examples use clearly stated probabilities.

## Audio intent
Narration is the primary audio layer. Use a restrained electronic bed at low gain, with a soft pulse when the possibility count doubles. Add a brief tick for each binary question and a warm resolve tone when the equation appears. Duck the bed under equations and spoken definitions. Keep the mix intelligible on laptop speakers and headphones.

## Editorial safeguards
Do not imply that uncertainty is a feeling or that bits are stored only in computers. Avoid presenting a binary digit and a bit of information as interchangeable without context. Keep the coin fair in the introductory example, and label the equal-likelihood assumption before generalizing. Any analogy must return to the measurable question: how many equally likely possibilities were ruled out?

### Expanded example notes`nA sealed box begins with eight equally likely symbols. Revealing one yes or no answer removes half the symbols. Revealing a second answer removes half again. The narration should count the surviving set aloud so the viewer feels the exponential shrinkage before seeing a formula.

### Terminology notes`nDefine uncertainty as the number of plausible alternatives under the stated model. Define information as the amount learned when an outcome is revealed. Define a bit as the amount learned from one fair binary choice. Keep these definitions stable across narration, captions, and graphics.

### Counterexample notes`nShow that a biased coin does not make heads and tails equally informative. The point is not to derive entropy in full, but to prevent the viewer from mistaking the fair coin setup for a universal rule. Add a spoken qualifier and a small probability label.

### Compression connection`nUse a short message with repeated symbols to motivate efficient coding. Common outcomes can receive short code words when the code respects the distribution. Make clear that this follows from expected information and is not a claim that every file compresses.

### Communication connection`nA sender chooses one message from a known set and the receiver learns which one arrived. Noise can obscure the answer, so practical systems add redundancy. Keep this connection brief and reserve channel capacity details for another episode.

### Visual metaphor notes`nUse a grid of possibilities, a narrowing beam, and a count that halves at each question. Avoid decorative data streams that do not encode a claim. Every transition should reveal a relationship between count, question, and uncertainty.

### Caption notes`nCaptions should preserve mathematical symbols where possible and spell out “log base two” on first use. Break lines at clause boundaries. Keep equations on their own line when the renderer supports it, and ensure that punctuation matches narration.

### Accessibility notes`nNarration must carry the logic even if color or motion is unavailable. Use high contrast for the possibility grid, avoid relying on hue alone, and state the direction of change in words. Keep animated counts readable at reduced motion settings.

### Fact check notes`nReview the base of the logarithm, the unit name, the equal probability assumption, and the distinction between self-information and entropy. Record the checking date and source locations in the production archive.
